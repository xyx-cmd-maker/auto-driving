Abstract
29

Automated truck platooning is a promising technology that is expected to be mainstream within
30
the next decade. For the foreseeable future, automated truck platoons will coexist and interact with
31
human drivers. Resembling a train-like formation, automated truck platoons may present challenges
32
for drivers wishing to overtake them, and it is not currently clear how these new formations affect
33
driver behaviour. Therefore, this paper aims to examine driver behaviours in the overtaking process
34
under various platoon organisations. A high-fidelity driving simulation experiment was conducted
35
to investigate the influence of platoon speed (80km/h and 100km/h), size (three trucks and five
36
trucks), inner gap (5m and 25m) and the surrounding traffic situation, e.g., the presence of a lead
37
vehicle, on drivers’ overtaking behaviour. Thirty-eight participants were recruited in the experiment.
38
Results revealed that compared to 80km/h conditions, the 100km/h conditions prompted more
39
drivers to exhibit extreme behaviours—either failing to overtake or performing a risky overtaking.
40
Regarding platoon size, drivers tended to deviate farther from the lane center to maintain a larger
41
lateral distance from the platoon under five-truck conditions. With respect to the inner gap, a 25m
42
inner gap significantly reduced the proportion of successful overtaking maneuvers. Moreover, in
43
critical conditions, a 5m inner gap extended drivers’ response time but did not significantly impact
44
collision probability. The presence of the lead vehicle increased drivers’ mental workload and
45
impaired longitudinal stability. These findings may offer insights for managing automated truck
46
platoons. For instance, the platoon speed and inner gap can be regulated in different traffic
47
conditions, to optimize efficiency, while ensuring safety for all road users.
48

49
Keywords: automated truck platooning, driving simulator, driver behaviour, overtaking

1. Introduction
51
Connected and autonomous vehicle (CAV) technology has made significant progress and holds
52
the potential to revolutionize the transportation system. One of the most promising CAV
53
technologies is automated truck platooning (Ma et al., 2022). Unlike automated vehicles (AVs) that
54
solely rely on sensor data, Dedicated Short-Range Communication (DSRC) technology enables
55
multiple trucks to virtually link with each other, maintaining a constant and short inner gap within
56
the platoon. This inner gap can be reduced to about 5m at a speed of 80km/h (Tsugawa, 2014). This
57
close arrangement effectively reduces fuel consumption, emissions, and promotes traffic efficiency
58
(Fagnant & Kockelman, 2015; Hussein & Rakha, 2021; Tsugawa et al., 2016). Additionally, it has
59
been proposed that automated truck platooning can alleviate the workload of the truck drivers. Some
60
cutting-edge platooning technologies can even achieve unmanned operation for the following trucks
61
(Watanabe et al., 2021). It is proposed that less involvement of human drivers significantly reduces
62
the risk of human-factors-related accidents. Automated truck platooning has undergone successful
63
testing on public roads in various countries and is expected to be introduced to the market within
64
the next decade (Fagnant & Kockelman, 2015).
65
Despite the numerous benefits, the introduction of automated truck platoons can also give rise
66
to a range of issues. One major concern is the interaction between manual vehicles (MVs) and
67
automated truck platoons (Axelsson, 2017). Given that mixed traffic of autonomous vehicles (AVs)
68
and MVs is an essential step in the development of automated driving technologies, it is inevitable
69
that manual drivers will share the road with automated truck platoons for the foreseeable future
70
(Guhathakurta & Kumar, 2019). However, when several trucks create long formation exceeding 100
71
meters in length, they can present a perplexing scenario for other road users, which is not currently
72
well-known by the average road user. Consequently, other road users may respond unexpectedly to
73
this new system, a phenomenon termed 'behavioural adaptation' (OECD, 1990). To ensure
74
compatibility between automated truck platoons and the transportation system, it is imperative to
75
fully explore the behavioural adaptation of drivers in MVs, using the same road space as automated
76
truck platoons.
77
While previous studies have examined behavioural adaptation during manual driving to
78
automated truck platoons in various driving processes (Chen et al., 2022; Gouy et al., 2014; Lee et
79

===== PAGE 5 =====

al., 2018; Razmi Rad et al., 2021), the overtaking process has been overlooked. Although overtaking
80
only lasts a few seconds, it is a complex procedure requiring accurate situation judgment and flexible
81
operation (Yang et al., 2018). On freeways, overtaking poses the risk of rear-end and lateral
82
collisions. Statistics from the Ministry of Public Safety of China show that overtaking accidents
83
account for about 30% of accidents on freeways (Ministry of Public Safety of China, 2021).
84
Compared to overtaking a car, the risk of accidents can further increase when overtaking a truck.
85
Existing studies have found that even a single truck can lead to changes in the driving behaviour of
86
surrounding drivers (Figueira & Larocca, 2020), their attention allocation (Zhang et al., 2016) and
87
mental workload (de Waard et al., 2009), during the overtaking process. Due to its length, an
88
automated truck platoon may exacerbate these impacts and result in unexpected behaviours of
89
surrounding drivers during overtaking.
90
Therefore, to ensure the safety of mixed traffic, it is crucial to understand how automated truck
91
platoons influence surrounding drivers’ behaviours during overtaking. This study aims to examine
92
the effects of platoon organisation and traffic situations on the overtaking behaviours of nearby
93
manual vehicle drivers. The results of this study can provide practical implications for the
94
operational strategies of automated truck platoons.
95
1.1 Impacts of trucks on other drivers’ overtaking behaviours
96
Accidents involving trucks contribute to approximately one-third of China’s total traffic
97
accidents on freeway (Ministry of Public Safety of China, 2021). In the USA, this number is
98
approximately 50% (National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 2023). One of the primary
99
reasons behind this high ratio is the conspicuous size disparity between trucks and cars. This
100
discrepancy becomes especially pronounced when other vehicles attempt to overtake a truck.
101
Drivers not only contend with a lengthier longitudinal overtaking distance but also endure lateral
102
pressure from the truck, due to its size and weight. This disparity can not only escalate the mental
103
workload of overtaking drivers, but also possibly alter their driving behaviors, resulting in greater
104
speed variability and reduced minimum time headway (de Waard et al., 2009; 2008).
105
Extensive research has explored the impact of trucks or long combination vehicles on the
106
behaviour of other drivers during the overtaking process. Drivers have been found to exhibit more
107

===== PAGE 6 =====

hesitation (Barton & Morrall, 1998) and frustration (Sabek et al., 2024) when preparing to
108
overtake a truck compared to overtaking a car. Surprisingly, however, drivers have shown an even
109
stronger willingness to overtake when confronted with a truck in comparison to a car (Gao et al.,
110
2023).
111
In terms of driving behaviour, overtaking a truck has been shown to significantly increase both
112
the time and distance required for overtaking, thereby elevating the risk of collisions with oncoming
113
vehicles on two-lane highways (Hanley & Forkenbrock, 2005). Figueira and Larocca (2020)
114
observed that a truck prompted drivers to maintain a larger following distance at the beginning of
115
the overtaking maneuver. Furthermore, studies have investigated the impact of overtaking a truck
116
on drivers’ gaze behaviour. Zhang et al. (2016b) discovered that the presence of a truck led to a
117
significant increase in both the duration and frequency of drivers’ general gaze during the overtaking
118
process.
119
The above studies have shed some light on how a single truck influences drivers’ overtaking
120
behaviour. However, when multiple trucks form a platoon, they collectively introduce a new
121
dynamic to other drivers on the road. The platoon’s exceptionally elongated length and
122
unconventional arrangement may intensify the pressure felt by drivers, thereby influencing their
123
decision-making and potentially leading to different driving behaviours during overtaking
124
maneuvers. Consequently, it is imperative to examine how other drivers respond to automated truck
125
platoons during the overtaking process.
126
1.2 Behavioural adaptations of human drivers to automated vehicle platoons
127
Due to its composition of multiple vehicles in close proximity, an automated vehicle platoon
128
stands out conspicuously on the road, setting it apart from traditionally human-driven vehicles.
129
Consequently, other drivers may exhibit different behaviours when interacting with automated
130
vehicle platoons compared to interacting with human-driven vehicles, including changes in speed
131
management, following strategies, and more. This shift in behaviour can be classified as
132
'behavioural adaptation,' denoting “unintended change in the behavior of the users with the
133
introduction of a new system against the system’s intended designed operation” (OECD, 1990).
134
Numerous studies have explored the behavioural adaptations of drivers to automated vehicle
135

===== PAGE 7 =====

platoons using driving simulation. The number of trucks in a platoon as well as the inner gap of the
136
platoon, are considered key influencing factors in affecting surrounding drivers’ behaviour. Among
137
these, the car-following process has been extensively examined (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Gouy et al.,
138
2014, 2013; Razmi Rad et al., 2021; Suh et al., 2017). Results show that drivers tend to mimic the
139
close arrangement of the platoon and adopt smaller time headways during car-following, when
140
compared to their usual behaviour (Gouy et al., 2013; Razmi Rad et al., 2021). This tendency
141
becomes more pronounced as the inner gap of the platoon decreases (i.e., trucks in the platoon
142
operate within a shorter distance to each other) (Chen et al., 2022; Gouy et al., 2014). Furthermore,
143
a smaller inner gap is seen to intensify drivers’ effort and alertness (Gouy et al., 2014). In addition
144
to car-following, some studies have investigated the influence of automated vehicle platoons on the
145
lane-changing behaviour of nearby drivers. The presence of automated vehicle platoons prompts
146
drivers to engage in riskier lane-changing behaviours, such as accepting smaller gaps and making
147
more drastic swerves (Razmi Rad et al., 2021; Trende et al., 2019). Moreover, the inner gap changes
148
drivers’ attention allocation during the lane change process (Guo et al., 2019). A few studies have
149
also focused on the merging and diverging behaviours of drivers when interacting with automated
150
vehicle platoons, and show that the size and inner gap of the platoon are contributing factors
151
influencing the merging and diverging behaviours of surrounding drivers (Aramrattana et al., 2022;
152
Wei et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). In addition, the recognizability of platoon significantly affects
153
driving safety during the merging and diverging process of other vehicles (Sultana and Hassan,
154
2024).
155
Studies above have offered valuable insights into how drivers adapt their behaviour when
156
interacting with automated vehicle platoons across various scenarios (e.g, car-following, lane-
157
changing, merging and diverging). However, there has been comparatively less focus on the
158
implications of automated vehicle platoons for the overtaking safety of other drivers. Particularly
159
for truck platoons, their unique characteristics, resembling long and train-like formations, pose
160
significant challenges for drivers attempting to overtake them. Using numerical computation, some
161
studies have demonstrated a significant reduction in sight distance caused by automated truck
162
platoons on freeways (Brewer & Fitzpatrick, 2017; Garcia & Pastor‐Serrano, 2022; Haq et al., 2022;
163
Thomas & Martinez-Perez, 2015). A study by Song (2024), investigating the collision risk
164
associated with overtaking truck platoons revealed an increased risk of collision with longer platoon
165

===== PAGE 8 =====

lengths or for higher speeds. These studies suggest that the organisation of the platoon, especially
166
the number of trucks used and the inner gap between these, should be carefully considered to ensure
167
overtaking safety by the surrounding traffic.
168
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only one existing study has investigated the influence of
169
platoon size on drivers’ acceptance of overtaking trucks (Larburu et al., 2010). However, this study
170
primarily focused on participants’ subjective feelings, overlooking their driving behaviours.
171
Additionally, the platoon model in this study consisted of a leading truck and over ten following
172
cars, differing significantly from prevalent automated truck platoons. Therefore, to ensure the safe
173
deployment of automated truck platoons, there is a strong need for research into the influence of
174
automated truck platoons on other drivers’ behaviours, during overtaking.
175
1.3 Research gap and the current study
176
Based on the existing literature, we can conclude that extensive studies have been conducted to
177
unveil the impact of the organisation of automated vehicle platoons on nearby drivers. Additionally,
178
the impact of a single truck on drivers’ overtaking behaviour is also well studied. However, three
179
main research gaps remain. Firstly, the influence of automated truck platoons on nearby drivers’
180
behaviours during the overtaking process is overlooked. Due to their sheer length, and an operating
181
process that is not yet experienced on the real road, it can be argued that automated truck platoons
182
may pose a big challenge for drivers who intend to overtake these formations. Therefore, the
183
organisation of automated truck platoons should be carefully managed for the safety of mixed traffic.
184
Secondly, the synergistic effect of multiple platoon-organisational factors has not been thoroughly
185
studied. While existing research has explored individual factors, no study has holistically examined
186
how these factors interact to influence drivers' behaviours. Thirdly, while it is known that the platoon
187
organisation may cause a difference in drivers’ alertness (Gouy et al., 2014) and visual attention
188
allocation (Guo et al., 2019), the extent to which these differences impact drivers’ response and
189
collision risk in critical situations remains unclear. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate how
190
different organisations of automated truck platoons affect drivers' behaviours during overtaking in
191
both non-critical and critical scenarios.
192
The main objective of this study was to investigate the influence of an automated truck
193

===== PAGE 9 =====

platoon’s organisation on the road, on the overtaking behaviour of nearby drivers. Based on previous
194
studies, the platoon’s speed, size, and inner gap are considered as potential influencing factors. In
195
addition to platoon organisations, the presence of a lead vehicle (LV) in the adjacent lane to the
196
platoon was also considered to create a more realistic traffic situation.
197
The hypotheses of the study were:
198
H1: A higher number of trucks in the platoon, a higher platoon speed, and the presence of the
199
LV will result in fewer overtaking maneuvers by drivers.
200
H2: A shorter inner gap within the platoon will prompt drivers to imitate and adopt smaller time
201
headways to the LV. In conditions without an LV, this imitation will manifest as a higher average
202
speed.
203
H3: A higher number of trucks in the platoon will result in a higher mental workload for drivers
204
(H3a), maintaining a larger lateral distance to the platoon (H3b).
205
H4: Decreased inner gap will result in a higher mental workload for drivers(H4a), maintaining a
206
larger lateral distance to the platoon (H4b), and reacting more quickly in critical situations (H4c).
207
The main innovation of the current study is its exploration of how the organisation of automated
208
truck platoons impacts the behaviours of other drivers during one of the riskiest scenarios—the
209
overtaking process. The findings from this study can provide valuable insights for designing the
210
organisation of automated truck platoons.
211
2. Methodology
212
2.1 Participants
213

To calculate the sample size for the statistical analysis, a power analysis was conducted in
214
G*Power. This analysis utilized a power of 0.80, an alpha level of 0.05, and effect sizes of large
215
magnitude (f=0.40 for repeated measures ANOVA, and w=0.60 for a Pearson chi-square test).
216
According to the assumptions above, the sample size for each respective statistical analysis method
217
were determined as follows: 12 for repeated measures ANOVA and 22 for the Pearson chi-square
218
test.
219
A total of 38 participants were recruited for the study through Internet. However, 3 participants
220

===== PAGE 10 =====

abandoned the study due to driving simulation sickness. Therefore, 35 participants (22 males and
221
13 females) completed the experiment. All participants had valid driving licenses and normal or
222
corrected-to-normal vision. The participants were aged between 21 to 52 years old (M = 28.1 years,
223
SD = 6.3 years), and their driving experience varied between 2 to 18 years (M = 6.9 years, SD = 3.4
224
years). The ethical approval was unavailable due to the absence of an Ethics Committee at Tongji
225
university. However, authors extensively deliberated on the experiment ’s risks beforehand and
226
scrutinized the experimental procedures carefully to ensure the safety and the ethical feasibility of
227
the experiment. Before the experiment, participants were informed that the data usage was solely
228
restricted to this program. Additionally, the potential risk of simulator sickness and the privacy
229
protection policy were disclosed to them. All participants gave consent to participate in this study.
230
2.2 Apparatus
231

The Tongji University driving simulator was utilized in this study, comprising both hardware
232
and software components (Figure 1). The hardware system incorporates an eight-degree-of-freedom
233
motion system operating on 20 × 5 m XY rails, an immersive 5-channel projection system offering
234
a front image view spanning 250° × 40° at a resolution of 1000 × 1050, refreshed at 60 Hz.
235
Additionally, it includes a vehicle motion control system featuring a fully equipped Megane III
236
vehicle, installed within the cab of the simulation platform. The motion system enables 28.6, 15.6,
237
and 6.95 m payload envelopes in the X, Y, and Z directions, respectively, with a maximum
238
acceleration of 6.5 m/s2 in all motion directions. The software utilized for rendering the road
239
environment is the SCANeRTM Studio 2023, which encompasses modules for scene modeling,
240
vehicle parameters, scenario scripting, experimental simulation, and data analysis. The simulator's
241
data sampling rate was set at 10 Hz.

2.3 Driving environment
245

The simulated roadway comprised a typical two-lane Chinese highway with a speed limit of
246
120km/h. We deliberately limited the lanes to create a narrower visual setting and intensify the
247
feeling of confinement for our drivers, induced by the truck platoon. A dual crash barrier separated
248
the two carriageways, with a median divider width of 3 meters and each lane measuring 3.75 meters
249
in width. Moderate-density traffic flow was implemented on the opposing carriageway to enhance
250
realism.
251

The total length of the road model was 19.8km, as depicted in Figure 2. It comprised of four
252
straight segments, with each pair of adjacent straight segments being separated by a transition
253
segment consisting of curves and transition curves. To ensure that the road geometry did not
254
influence drivers’ overtaking behaviour, all overtaking maneuvers were conducted within the
255
straight segments. Each straight segment was 2.4 km in length, consistent with the maximum
256
allowable length of straight sections specified in China’s highway design code. This length was
257
chosen because, if drivers were unable to overtake the truck platoon within this distance, it would
258
be nearly impossible for them to do so in real-life scenarios. The curves all had radii exceeding
259
1400m, and the road slope varied between -1.5% and +1.5%, striking a balance between creating a
260
realistic road environment and ensuring moderate difficulty in vehicle manipulation for drivers.
261
Other aspects of the road model adhered to the specifications outlined in China’s highway design
262
code.
263

The ego-vehicle model in the simulation environment was a Renault Megane III, with
264

===== PAGE 12 =====

dimensions of 4.30 meters in length, 1.80 meters in width, and 1.47 meters in height. Its engine
265
provided a maximum power output of 104kW. The trucks comprising the platoon were semi-trailers,
266
each measuring 13.0 meters in length, 2.50 meters in width, and 3.65 meters in height. Driving
267
scenes from the perspective of the ego vehicle are shown as Figure 3.

276
A repeated measures, within-subject, design was employed in this study, incorporating two
277
distinct scenarios: non-critical and critical. The non-critical scenarios aimed to explore the influence
278
of platoon organisation and traffic situations on drivers’ overtaking behaviour in routine conditions,
279
while the critical scenarios sought to assess the impact of platoon organisation on drivers’ response
280
to emergencies.

283
for the non-critical scenarios. Various platoon organisations are illustrated as Figure 4. The detailed
284
specifications of these factors are as follows:
285
Platoon speed: This factor consisted of two levels - 80km/h and 100km/h. 100km/h was set as
286
the upper level because it is the speed limit for trucks on freeways according to China’s road traffic
287
safety law.
288
Platoon size: Reflecting the number of trucks within the platoon, the platoon size had two
289
levels: three and five trucks. Past field experiments (SAIC, 2020; Watanabe et al., 2021; Yang et al.,
290
2021) have demonstrated variability in the number of trucks, typically ranging from three to five.
291
Additionally, research by Castritius et al. (2020) suggested that a maximum of five vehicles received
292
the highest level of public acceptance.
293
Platoon Inner Gap: Representing the distance between two adjacent trucks in the platoon, this
294
factor was set at two levels: 5 m. and 25 m. Existing field tests have shown that the minimum inner
295
gap achieved at high speed (exceeding 80km/h) is around 5m (Shladover, 2007; Tsugawa, 2014).
296
Thus, 5m was set as the lower level. However, under certain conditions where fast and precise
297
communication cannot be guaranteed, an increase in the inner gap may be necessary. Based on
298
recent projects with relatively long test distances, an inner gap of 25m was deemed robust enough
299
at high speeds (Castritius et al., 2021; SAIC, 2020).
300
Presence of the LV: Traffic situations can significantly influence drivers’ overtaking behaviour.
301
This factor distinguishes between scenarios with and without an LV. In scenarios with the LV, a
302
vehicle was positioned in front of the ego vehicle during the overtaking process. Conversely, in
303
scenarios without the LV, the ego vehicle was the sole vehicle alongside the truck platoon.
304
For critical scenarios, a 2 (platoon size: three trucks, five trucks) ×2 (platoon inner gap: 5 m.,
305
25 m.) design was implemented. We did not use an equal number of non-critical and critical
306
scenarios for two main reasons. First, to prevent participants from developing a learning effect on
307
the critical event. Repeated exposure could make drivers anticipate it, potentially altering their
308
natural responses. Second, to control the total experiment duration. A prolonged experiment leads
309
to driver fatigue and simulation sickness, which could affect the results. Across all critical scenarios,
310
the platoon maintained a speed of 100 km/h. Moreover, the LV was consistently present in all critical
311
scenarios. The key distinction between critical and non-critical scenarios lies in the behavior of the
312

===== PAGE 14 =====

LV: in non-critical scenarios, the LV did not engage in any abrupt deceleration throughout the
313
overtaking process, whereas in critical scenarios, the LV executed an abrupt braking maneuver
314
midway through the overtaking.
315
In total, the experiment comprised a total of twenty scenarios, with sixteen non-critical
316
scenarios and four critical scenarios. The twenty scenarios were evenly distributed into five road
317
sections in a counterbalanced sequence. Participants encountered four scenarios in each road section.
318
To mitigate any learning effect, four critical scenarios were interspersed among the non-critical ones.
319
To prevent any carryover effect from the experimental sequence, the order of the five road sections
320
was randomized for each participant.
321
At the onset of each road section, the platoons were stationed statically in the right lane at the
322
start of straight segments, maintaining their predetermined size and inner gap. In scenarios with an
323
LV, a car was positioned statically in the left lane, trailing 200 meters behind the last truck of the
324
platoon. A designated triggering point initiated the motion of both the platoon and the LV, situated
325
500 meters behind the last truck. This distance was determined through a pre-test conducted before
326
the formal experiment to ensure that participants did not anticipate and adjust their speed
327
unexpectedly. Upon the ego vehicle reaching this point, all trucks within the platoon accelerated at
328
a rate of 1.5m/s² until reaching the preset speed, concurrently with the LV’s acceleration.
329
During the ego vehicle's approach, the LV's acceleration underwent phased changes designed
330
to mitigate the sense of abrupt acceleration and ensure that both the LV's speed and its distance from
331
the ego vehicle were well controlled. The LV's acceleration process can be divided into three phases:
332
1. Once the ego vehicle reached the triggering point, the LV would accelerate at 2.5
333
m/s² for 8 seconds, reaching a speed of 72 km/h. Then its speed would maintain
334
until the next acceleration phase. During this period, the distance between the LV
335
and the ego vehicle continuously decreased. This initial acceleration phase was
336
designed to bring the LV from a standstill to a relatively high speed.
337
2. When the distance between the ego vehicle and the LV decreased to 100 meters, the
338
system would record the ego vehicle's speed at that moment, setting the target speed
339
of the LV in this phase to be 3 m/s lower than the ego vehicle's speed. The system
340
would then calculate the required acceleration for the LV to reach this target speed
341
within 2 seconds. For example, if the ego vehicle's speed was 30 m/s when the
342

===== PAGE 15 =====

distance decreased to 100 meters, the target speed of the LV would be 27 m/s.
343
Consequently, the LV would accelerate at 3.5 m/s² for 2 seconds. After this, the LV
344
would maintain the target speed until the next acceleration phase. This second
345
acceleration phase helped the LV’s speed gradually approach that of the ego vehicle,
346
ensuring a smooth transition and preventing abrupt changes in the LV’s speed in the
347
following phase.
348
3. When the distance between the LV and the ego vehicle decreased to 75 meters, the
349
speed of the LV would immediately match that of the ego vehicle. From that point
350
onward, the LV would maintain synchronized speed with the ego vehicle. This phase
351
ensured that the two vehicles reached the expected state of dynamic equilibrium. Pre-
352
test results indicated that the LV's speed adjustment was smooth and did not cause
353
any discomfort or unnatural perception for the participant.
354
The final speed of the LV after acceleration completely dependent on the speed of the ego
355
vehicle. Once the distance between the LV and the ego vehicle decreased to 75 meters, the LV's
356
speed matched that of the ego vehicle, maintaining this synchrony thereafter. This means that in all
357
non-critical scenarios and in critical scenarios before the critical event was triggered, the speed of
358
the LV would change synchronously with that of the ego vehicle without any delay. The reason for
359
aligning the speed of the LV with that of the ego vehicle, instead of maintaining a constant speed,
360
was to deter certain participants from waiting until the LV had overtaken the platoon entirely before
361
initiating their own overtaking maneuvers. This approach aimed to preserve the influence exerted
362
by the LV on overtaking behaviour.
363
The difference between the non-critical and critical scenarios lies in whether an urgent brake
364
of the LV occurs. In non-critical scenarios with the LV, the LV maintained a constant distance from
365
the ego vehicle throughout the overtaking process, and the platoon’s speed remained constant.
366
However, in critical scenarios, when the ego vehicle aligned with the middle truck in the platoon
367
(i.e., second truck for three-truck platoons and third truck for five-truck platoons), the LV would
368
decelerate at 5m/s² until reaching 70km/h. Simultaneously, the platoon decelerated at a rate of 2m/s²
369
until reaching 60km/h. Participants needed to implement countermeasures promptly to avert
370
potential collisions. Before the LV initiated the urgent brake, the participant was unable to
371
distinguish whether the current scenario was critical or non-critical.
372

===== PAGE 16 =====

If the ego vehicle had not overtaken the first truck of the platoon in non-critical scenarios or
373
triggered the deceleration of the LV in critical scenarios by the time the first truck of the platoon
374
reached the end of the straight section, the platoon would decelerate to 60 km/h. Furthermore, if
375
there was an LV, it would go to the right lane and decelerate, facilitating the ego vehicle’s overtaking
376
maneuver. Nevertheless, this overtaking attempt would be classified as incomplete.

As described earlier, the truck platoon operates as a significant presence on the road, potentially

409
of participants who successfully completed overtaking and the total number of participants. In
410

411

The indicators for longitudinal driving performance evaluation included mean velocity (Vmean)
414
and standard deviation of velocity (Vstd). Vmean offered insights into the vehicle’s longitudinal
415
motion state during a period, while Vstd could represent the fluctuation of the vehicle’s speed (Chen
416
et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023). Indicators considered for lateral driving performance were mean
417
lateral position (LPmean), standard deviation of lateral position (SDLP) and maximum lateral
418
acceleration (LAmax). LPmean represented the lateral offset to the centerline during a period, reflecting
419
the risk of lateral collision. SDLP represents the stability of lane keeping, which can be used to
420
evaluate the lateral overtaking stability (Chen et al., 2022; Gouy et al., 2014). LAmax is good
421
indicator of the jerkiness of the vehicle motion and can evaluate the vehicle’s lateral stability in
422
lane-changing process (Madigan et al., 2018).
423

For critical scenarios, the indicators included the response time (RT) and the minimum time to
424
collision (TTCmin). As outlined in the introduction, different platoon formations might influence
425
drivers’ alertness and attention, thus affecting their response capability. RT was chosen to evaluate
426
drivers’ response ability across various platoon organisation scenarios. It was calculated as the time
427
interval between the initiation of braking by the LV and the detection of a brake pedal application
428
exceeding 10% (note that the steering wheel maneuver was not considered as all drivers initially
429
applied the brakes rather than changing lanes). TTCmin is an effective metric for assessing the
430
severity of potential collisions, calculated by dividing the distance gap between the ego-vehicle and
431
the LV by their relative speed. A low value of TTCmin indicated a high potential of a rear-end
432
collision with the LV (Louw et al., 2017).
433

The NASA-TLX (Task Load Index) (Hart & Staveland, 1988) was implemented to evaluate
434
drivers’ subjective mental workload after each overtaking. NASA-TLX has six sub-scales: mental
435
demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration, and participants
436
needed to score every sub-scale from 0 to 10. This process was conducted orally. The total NASA-
437
TLX was calculated as a weighted sum. The higher the NASA-TLX, the higher the mental workload
438
felt by the participant.
439
As shown in Figure 5, four points were used to represent the different stages in the overtaking
440
process. The overtaking start denoted the moment when the lateral position of the vehicle increased
441
continuously for 3 seconds, and the lane-keeping start denoted the moment when the maintained
442
lateral position remained constant or started to decrease for the next one second (Soni et al., 2020).
443

===== PAGE 19 =====

Correspondingly, the lane-keeping end denoted the moment when the lateral position decreased
444
continuously for 3 seconds, and the overtaking end denoted the moment when the lateral position
445
was constant or started to increase for the next one second. Following extraction, these points were
446
manually inspected to ensure accuracy. As a result, three sections were defined by these points:
447
overtaking start section, overtaking straight section, and overtaking end section. Vmean and Vstd were
448
examined respectively for all three sections. Considering that the lane change was a dynamic process,
449
we only selected the overtaking straight section as the time window for LPmean and SDLP. The time
450
window for LAmax covered the lane-changing process, which included overtaking start section and
451
overtaking end section.

4. Discussion
680

This driving simulation study was used to investigate the effects of speed, size, and inner gap
681
of an automated truck platoon, and the presence of the LV on the overtaking behaviour of drivers.
682
In the following sections, the results are discussed based on each dependent variable.
683
4.1 Effect of platoon speed on overtaking behaviours
684

The increase in platoon speed resulted in a substantial decrease in OCR, consistent with H1.
685
A previous study in this context found that the speed of the impeding vehicle is a primary factor
686
affecting drivers’ motivation to overtake (Kinnear et al., 2015). In this study, higher platoon
687
speeds not only dampened drivers’ willingness to overtake, but also increased the difficulty of
688
overtaking maneuvers, thus resulting in a lower OCR.
689
In addition, an escalation in platoon speed significantly increased Vmean in the overtaking
690
straight section and the overtaking end section. Vstd was also higher in the overtaking start section
691
and the overtaking straight section when the platoon speed increased, as was the subjective
692
workload, based on the NASA-TLX. This corroborates the findings of Chen et al. (2022), who
693
highlighted that a higher platoon speed would prompt drivers to adopt riskier longitudinal driving
694
behaviour, and impose a greater mental workload. It is important to note that, for the 80 km/h
695
platoons, an average travelling speed above the speed limit of 120km/h was only seen for about
696
30% of the total cases, while this increased to around 44% for the 100km/h conditions. This
697
implies that the increase in platoon speed may heighten drivers’ concern that they cannot complete
698
overtaking within straight sections, leading a higher proportion of them to exceed the speed limit.
699
It is important to note that we have adopted the maximum allowable length of straight section in
700
this study to facilitate overtaking. In reality, such lengthy straight segments are uncommon.
701
Consequently, drivers might adopt higher speeds than those observed in this study to overtake a
702
truck platoon, thereby further increasing the risk of accidents.
703
Although the platoon speed was the most significant influencing factor on Vmean, it is worth
704

===== PAGE 36 =====

noting that overtaking start section was the only section in which Vmean was not significantly
705
affected by the platoon speed. This indicates that during the initial stage of overtaking, it was hard
706
for drivers to perceive the platoon speed accurately in this driving simulator study, and they did
707
not increase their speed promptly or appropriately to match the higher platoon speed. This delayed
708
response to the platoon speed potentially causes drivers to adopt more radical driving maneuvers
709
to compensate, increasing the risk of accidents.
710
Overall, the results indicated that a higher platoon speed induced extreme driving
711
behaviours—which either included failed overtaking attempts, or risky overtaking maneuvers,
712
thereby compromising traffic efficiency and elevating the likelihood of accidents. Therefore, it is
713
imperative to consider the speed limit for automated truck platoons, advocating for a limit below
714
the current 100km/h standard for trucks in China, to facilitate safer overtaking opportunities for
715
other drivers.
716
4.2 Effect of platoon size on overtaking behaviours
717
A significant interaction effect was found between platoon size and inner gap, for the Vmean,
718
during the overtaking start section and the overtaking straight section. Under the three-truck
719
conditions, Vmean remained relatively consistent across the various inner gaps. However, in the
720
five-truck conditions, Vmean was notably higher with a 25m inner gap, when compared to a 5m
721
inner gap. One possible interpretation is that the inner gap’s contribution to the platoon’s total
722
length increased as the platoon size increased. With a 5m inner gap, transitioning from three to
723
five trucks only added 40m to the platoon's length. While with a 25m inner gap, this transition
724
added 80m to the platoon's length. Faced with a more substantial increase in platoon length,
725
drivers were more likely to be concerned about completing their overtaking maneuvers, leading
726
them to increase their speed. Thus, the stability of the traffic flow was compromised for these
727
conditions. This finding is consistent with a previous simulation-based study (Zhou and Zhu,
728
2021), which found that the increase of platoon length led to decreased traffic stability.
729
It is not surprising that an increase in the inner gap significantly added to OT. However, it is
730
noteworthy that the inner gap demonstrated a significant interaction effect with platoon size on
731
OT. Specifically, compared to 3-truck platoons, an inner gap of 25 m resulted in a greater increase
732

===== PAGE 37 =====

in OT with 5-truck platoons. While we previously discussed how the combination of a large
733
platoon size and a high inner gap extended the total length of the platoon, prompting drivers to
734
increase their speed to facilitate overtaking, it appears that even with the risk of speeding, the
735
negative impact of the increased length on traffic efficiency could not be offset. The combination
736
of a large platoon size and a high inner gap not only compromised the safety of mixed traffic but
737
also degraded overall traffic efficiency (Faber et al., 2020). Therefore, careful consideration of the
738
total length of the platoon is essential to mitigate the risk of speeding among other drivers and to
739
enhance the efficiency of mixed traffic.
740

When overtaking the three-truck platoons, the vehicle's lateral position deviated
741
approximately 10 cm further towards the median divider and away from the platoon, when
742
compared to the five-truck platoon conditions. This result is in line with H3. Previous studies have
743
indicated that increasing the size of the platoon makes other drivers to be less willing to drive
744
alongside them (Castritius et al., 2020; Larburu et al., 2010), prompting them to keep a larger
745
lateral distance to the platoon to achieve risk compensation (Rudin-Brown and Jamson, 2013).
746
This phenomenon reflected the lateral behavioural adaptation of manual drivers to automated
747
truck platoons. Although a lateral deviation of 10cm might appear negligible, it's important to note
748
that this deviation could vary depending on factors such as road geometry or surface
749
conditions(Chen et al., 2018). For example, in curved sections, the deviation may increase as the
750
curve radius decreases. In these conditions, behavioural adaptation may heighten the risk of side-
751
swipes, necessitating increased attention.
752
4.3 Effects of inner gap on overtaking behaviours
753

754

Compared with an inner gap of 25 m, an inner gap of 5 m led to higher LAmax in both
755
overtaking start section and overtaking end section. This finding mirrors the results of a previous
756
study, which indicated that drivers tend to adopt more radical steering maneuvers when changing
757
lane to a lane occupied by a platoon, compared with changing lane into a lane without a platoon
758
(Lee & Oh, 2017). This phenomenon can be attributed to the behavioural adaptation to truck
759
platoons. When encountering platoons with a small inner gap, drivers may perceive the gap for
760

===== PAGE 38 =====

lane change in the target lane as also small, prompting them to conduct the steering maneuver
761
hastily, consequently resulting in higher LAmax.
762

In critical scenarios, RT under 25m conditions was smaller than that under 5m conditions,
763
contradicting our hypothesis H4c. Additionally, RT was not significantly correlated with NASA-
764
TLX scores, suggesting that high mental workload did not account for the prolonged response
765
time. One possible explanation for the extended response time is that an 5m inter-vehicle gap is
766
exceptionally rare in most driving conditions, while a 25m gap is relatively common (Chen et al.,
767
2022). The distinctiveness of the small inter-vehicle gap likely compelled drivers to allocate extra
768
attention to it, thereby slowing their response. As shown in Figure 23, the interaction effect
769
between the inner gap and platoon size on RT could support this explanation. With an increase in
770
platoon size, the exposure time is prolonged, exacerbating behavioural adaptation (Razmi Rad et
771
al., 2021). Consequently, compared to three-truck conditions, drivers faced greater distraction
772
from the smaller inner gap in five-truck conditions. Therefore, the impact of the inner gap on RT
773
was more significant in five-truck conditions. However, the inner gap showed no significant effect
774
on TTCmin. Furthermore, collision cases were distributed almost evenly across conditions with
775
different inner gaps—two in 5m conditions and three in 25m conditions. This suggests that
776
although drivers' responses were slowed down by the smaller inner gap, they were still able to
777
implement effective countermeasures to compensate for it.
778
Compared to the negative impact of a 5m inner gap on RT and LAmax, a 25m gap seemed to
779
impose adverse effects substantially on drivers’ overtaking behaviour. The 25m inner gap
780
decreased the OCR and caused drivers to deviate farther from the center of the lane. This result
781
appeared to contradict existing literature, which suggested that a bigger inner gap is more
782
beneficial for other drivers’ performance (Chen et al., 2022; Gouy et al., 2014). However, it is
783
important to note our study focused on overtaking scenarios, whereas previous studies primarily
784
examined car-following situations. Consequently, the disparate results indicate that different
785
platoon organisations should be adopted for different scenarios. For example, if the density of the
786
platoon is high or a dedicated lane for platoons is implemented, then car-following would
787
predominate in most conditions. In such cases, increasing the inner gap can help mitigate the
788
behavioural adaptation of close car-following of other drivers. Conversely, when the density of the
789
platoon is low or the dedicated lane is not implemented, decreasing the inner gap would be
790

===== PAGE 39 =====

beneficial for improving traffic efficiency and other drivers’ overtaking performance, as well as
791
decreasing the possibility of other drivers’ cutting into the platoon (Aramrattana et al., 2022).
792
4.4 Effects of the presence of the LV on overtaking behaviours
793

The presence of the LV resulted in a higher LAmax in overtaking start section. A previous
794
study also revealed that higher traffic density caused more radical longitudinal behaviors during
795
the lane-changing process of overtaking (Yang et al., 2018). These findings indicate that higher
796
traffic density could lead drivers to engage in more reckless overtaking behaviours, potentially
797
compromising overtaking safety. Moreover, the interaction effect between platoon size and the
798
presence of the LV suggests that the impact of the LV on increasing drivers’ LAmax during lane-
799
changing process would be more significant in five-truck conditions. This phenomenon might be
800
explained by the relationship between the platoon length and the ego-vehicle’s distance to the LV.
801
When the platoon only contained three trucks, the platoon length was either 49m (5m inner gap)
802
or 89m (25m inner gap). Under these conditions, the LV was either beyond or almost parallel with
803
the first truck when the overtake began. This would reassure drivers, as they felt only laterally
804
suppressed and not completely trapped by the LV and the platoon. However, in five-platoon
805
conditions, the platoon length was either 85m (5m inner gap) or 165m (25m inner gap). Drivers
806
would feel completely trapped with no lateral or longitudinal space to handle any emergency.
807
Under these conditions, they would either gave up overtaking (when the platoon was 165m and
808
the LV was present, the OCR dropped to 75.7%), or become more nervous and behaved more
809
radically during overtaking. This result indicates that the total length of the platoon should be
810
managed considering traffic conditions. For example, the time headway with moderate traffic
811
density in highway is typically between 2.5 and 4 s (Evans & Wasielewski, 1983; Loulizi et al.,
812
2019). If the speed limit on the highway was 120 km/h, then the maximum platoon length
813
shouldn’t exceed 83m (distance travelled at 120 km/h for 2.5 s) to avoid the synergistic negative
814
effect of the platoon and the LV on drivers’ mental workload.
815
5. Limitations and future research
816

===== PAGE 40 =====

In terms of limitations, for practical reasons, this study only investigated overtaking
817
behaviors during straight segments. However, road geometry can also influence drivers’
818
behavioural adaptations. The complex interaction between road geometry and platoon organisation
819
may pose new challenges that need to be addressed in future research. Additionally, this study
820
only demonstrated that an inner gap of 5m was more beneficial for drivers’ overtaking behaviour
821
compared to an inner gap of 25m. However, the 5m inner gap may not be the optimal value. Thus,
822
future research could focus on determining the most suitable inner gap for automated truck
823
platoons by testing different inner gap sizes, such as 5m, 10m, 15m and 20m.
824
6. Conclusions
825

This exploratory study sheds light on how the organisation of truck platooning and the traffic
826
environment influence other drivers’ behaviours during the process of overtaking the platoon. It
827
could be concluded as follows:
828
(i)
The platoon speed was the most significant factor influencing drivers' longitudinal
829
behaviour. An increase in platoon speed resulted in higher average speeds and
830
greater speed fluctuations of the ego vehicle during the overtaking process, thereby
831
increasing the likelihood of speeding. However, the rise in platoon speed also
832
heightened the difficulty of overtaking—the overtaking completion rate was 7.5%
833
lower at a platoon speed of 100 km/h compared to 80 km/h.
834
(ii)
The inner gap of the platoon significantly affected drivers' lateral control. Compared
835
to a 25-meter inner gap, a 5-meter inner gap not only impaired lateral stability during
836
lane changes but also caused drivers to maintain a larger lateral distance from the
837
platoon during overtaking.
838
(iii)
In critical conditions, although drivers' response time to the lead vehicle's brake was
839
0.5 seconds slower in 5-meter conditions compared to 25-meter conditions, this did
840
not contribute to a higher crash probability.
841
(iv)
The presence of the LV imposes a significant mental workload on drivers. It
842
functioned as an obstruction, reducing the mean speed and increasing the speed
843
fluctuation of the ego vehicle, which consequently resulted in a lower overtaking
844

===== PAGE 41 =====

completion rate.
845
This study may provide suggestions for the management of automated truck platoons from a
846
human factor perspective. The operating speed of automated truck platoons should be lower than
847
the current speed limit for trucks. In terms of platoon organisations, different speeds and inner
848
gaps of the platoon should be adopted based on varying traffic conditions to strike a balance
849
between efficiency and safety.
