Abstract
17
Learning-based car-following control (LCC) of connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs)
18
is gaining significant attention with the advancement of computing power and data
19
accessibility. While the flexibility and large model capacity of model-free architecture
20
enable LCC to potentially outperform the model-based car-following (CF) model in
21
improving traffic efficiency and mitigating congestion, the generalizability of LCC for
22
traffic conditions different from the training environment/dataset is not well-understood.
23
This study seeks to explore the impact of stop-and-go traffic in the training dataset on the
24
generalizability of LCC. It uses the characteristics of lead vehicle trajectories to describe
25
stop-and-go traffic, and links the theory of identifiability (i.e., obtaining a unique parameter
26
estimation result using sensor measurements) to the generalizability of behavior cloning
27
(BC) and policy-based deep reinforcement learning (DRL). Correspondingly, the study
28
shows theoretically that: (i) stop-and-go traffic can enable the property of identifiability and
29
enhance the control performance of BC-based LCC in different traffic conditions; (ii) stop-
30
and-go traffic is not necessary for DRL-based LCC to generalize to different traffic
31
conditions; (iii) DRL-based LCC trained with only constant-speed lead vehicle trajectories
32
(not sufficient to ensure identifiability) can be generalized to different traffic conditions; and
33
(iv) stop-and-go traffic increases variance in the training dataset, which improves the
34
convergence of parameter estimation while negatively impacting the convergence of DRL to
35
the optimal control policy. Numerical experiments validate the above findings, illustrating
36
that BC-based LCC entails comprehensive training datasets for generalizing to different
37
traffic conditions, while DRL-based LCC can achieve generalization with simple free-flow
38
traffic training environments. This further suggests DRL as a more promising and cost-
39
effective LCC approach to reduce operational costs, mitigate traffic congestion, and enhance
40
safety and mobility, which can accelerate the deployment and acceptance of CAVs.
41

42
Keywords: Car-following control, system identification, behavior cloning, deep
43
reinforcement learning, generalizability

1 Introduction
1
Rapid advances in vehicle automation and communication technologies are enabling the car-
2
following (CF) control of connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs). The CAV CF control
3
automatically maintains safe spacing between vehicles and the desired operating speed without
4
drivers’ inputs, which can improve traffic efficiency, driving comfort, and safety. Existing
5
studies on CAV CF control methods can be categorized into model-based and learning-based
6
methods. Model-based methods include mathematically tractable models that are designed based
7
on the analysis of one or more performance metrics (e.g., stability, efficiency, comfort, safety,
8
etc.). For example, the feedback and feed-forward control gains of linear controllers can be
9
analytically bounded to ensure local stability, string stability, or robustness against disturbances
10
(Zhou and Ahn, 2019; Zhou et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). To further improve CF control
11
performance, model predictive controllers are developed, whose weighting matrices can be
12
analytically tuned to enhance convergence, asymptotic stability, and adjust emphasis on specific
13
performance metrics (e.g., collision avoidance, comfort, fuel efficiency, etc.) (Wang et al., 2019;
14
Zhou et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2022b). The parameters of CF models can be directly analyzed to
15
evaluate the tradeoffs between stability, traffic throughput, and safety (Talebpour and
16
Mahmassani, 2016; Xie et al., 2018; Li, 2022).
17
Learning-based methods deploy deep neural networks (DNNs) and/or other approximators
18
to factorize the control action (e.g., vehicle speed, acceleration). Correspondingly, DNNs are
19
iteratively trained using data sampled from the training dataset or environment. Due to the
20
complex structure of DNNs and the highly dynamic training process, tractable mathematical
21
analysis of a specific performance metric is (typically) infeasible for learning-based methods. On
22
the other hand, the DNNs’ complex structures and the mechanism of sampling from the
23
environment cause learning-based methods to have significant nonlinearity while providing
24
flexibility to potentially outperform model-based methods to mitigate congestion and smoothen
25
traffic flow (Zhu et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2021). In particular, with advanced computing resources
26
and data availability, learning-based car-following control (LCC) is gaining substantial attention
27
from the autonomous driving industry (Zhou et al., 2022a). There are two main categories of
28
LCC methods: (i) behavior cloning (BC)-based LCC which generates control decisions by
29
imitating the reference vehicle trajectories in the training dataset through supervised learning,
30
and (ii) deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based LCC which computes control decisions by
31
maximizing episode reward in the training environment (i.e., CF interactions given sensor-
32
measured lead vehicle trajectories).
33
In real-world operations, the major concern with LCC is its generalizability in terms of
34
achieving the desired control performance under different traffic congestion characteristics (i.e.,
35
speed perturbation type, congestion level). To enhance the generalizability of LCC, existing
36
studies focus on improving training algorithms and DNN structures. For example, physics-
37
informed learning method (Mo et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023) and regularized Markov decision
38
process (Geist et al., 2019) seek to regulate the weight updates of DNNs using reference physical
39
models so that the DNNs can approximate the generalizability of physical models. The liquid
40
time-constant neural network (Hasani et al., 2021) and neural ordinary differential equations
41
(ODEs) (Chen et al., 2018) replace intermediate layers/nodes of DNNs with approximated ODE
42
solutions to enable DNNs to preserve the generalizability of ODEs. However, LCCs with
43
improved training algorithms and DNN structures still suffer from the impacts of ill-conditioned
44

training dataset/environment (e.g., insufficient size, biased speed distribution, and perturbation
1
types), leading to undesired control performance in congestion mitigation. More importantly, the
2
impacts of the training dataset/environment on the generalizability of LCC are still not well-
3
understood. The fundamental question “Should stop-and-go traffic be included in the training of
4
LCC to mitigate traffic congestion during operations?” remains unanswered. This research gap
5
will limit the reliability of LCC in congestion mitigation and can significantly reduce the putative
6
benefits of CAVs in real-world operations. It can also lead to complicated training environments
7
and excessively large training datasets when training LCC, as more sampled training data will be
8
intuitively preferred to improve generalizability (which may be unnecessary and entail wastage
9
of computing and storage resources). Hence, it is imperative to understand the training
10
environments or datasets that enable LCC to achieve generalizable performance to mitigate
11
traffic congestion. This will further assist in simplifying the training environment and reduce the
12
training dataset size to improve training efficiency.
13
To address the aforementioned critical research gap, this study starts from the parameter
14
estimation problem to articulate the linkage between model fitting and DNN training, and then
15
leverages the fundamental theory in system identification to explain the impact of stop-and-go
16
traffic on the generalizability of LCC. It first articulates that stop-and-go traffic can enhance the
17
convergence of CF parameter estimation. To obtain a unique optimal solution in the CF
18
parameter estimation problem from sensor measurements (i.e., the property of identifiability), the
19
dataset of lead vehicle trajectories (i.e., position and speed of the lead vehicle) needs to satisfy a
20
sufficient persistently exciting (PE) order. Such a condition indicates that lead vehicle
21
trajectories need to display sufficient fluctuations to excite the full spectrum of CF behavior from
22
a dynamical system, so that the corresponding parameters can be obtained using parameter
23
estimation methods. This study proves that lead vehicle trajectories in stop-and-go traffic
24
containing oscillatory components with at least two angular frequencies can enable sufficient PE
25
order to realize identifiability in CF parameter estimation. Lead vehicle trajectories with greater
26
variance induced by larger oscillatory magnitude and frequency can enhance the PE condition
27
and accelerate the convergence of the estimated parameter.
28
Next, the study draws an analogy between CF parameter estimation and BC-based LCC to
29
analyze the impact of stop-and-go traffic on BC-based LCC. As both BC-based LCC and CF
30
parameter estimation aim to minimize the difference between the estimated/retrofitted trajectory
31
(produced by DNNs or CF models) and the ground-truth trajectory in the dataset (e.g.,
32
minimizing the root mean squared error or other error norms), lead vehicle trajectories enabling
33
identifiability in CF parameter estimation should also enable the BC-based LCC to obtain a
34
robust DNN to deal with different traffic conditions. This is because a CF model can adapt to
35
different traffic conditions, and correspondingly the trajectories enabling unique estimated
36
parameters of a CF model should be able to uncover the weights of DNNs that can achieve
37
similar generalizability as a CF model. Further, the study theoretically shows that the lead
38
vehicle trajectories in stop-and-go traffic influence the weights of a DNN in a similar way as the
39
regularization term in the loss function, which seeks to avoid the overfitting issue and enhances
40
generalizability and robustness under different traffic conditions.
41
On the other hand, as the DRL uses the lead vehicle trajectories to build the training
42
environment to update the weights of DNNs, these trajectories also greatly impact the
43
generalizability of DRL-based LCC. However, because DRL updates the weights of DNNs by
44
maximizing the expected discounted reward and executing exploration-exploitation on control
45

policy (i.e., the control strategy to determine the best control action based on the current state),
1
different weight updating mechanisms lead to different impacts of stop-and-go traffic on the
2
generalizability of DRL-based LCC. Remarkably, our study suggests that a training dataset with
3
stop-and-go traffic is unnecessary to achieve generalizability for DRL. This is because
4

5

6

7

8
convergence rate of the estimated parameters, they can generate large variance in the DRL
9
training process (i.e., reward function, Q-value approximation), preventing the DRL from
10
converging to optimal control policy. Numerical experiments illustrate that even lead vehicle
11
trajectories with constant speed levels in free-flow traffic (which is insufficient to enable the
12
property of identifiability) can enable the DRL to generalize to different traffic conditions. The
13
results indicate that we can simplify the training environments, sample vehicle trajectories with
14
smaller variance, balance the exploration-exploitation during training, and design appropriate
15
reward functions to achieve generalizability for DRL-based LCC.
16
The contributions of this study are threefold. First, the study illustrates the significant role of
17
stop-and-go traffic in building training datasets to improve the generalizability and control
18
performance of BC-based LCC. Second, the theoretical analysis indicates stop-and-go traffic is
19
unnecessary for DRL-based LCC to generalize for different traffic conditions and suggests that
20
exploration mechanisms can enhance the generalizability of DRL-based LCC. Third, the study
21
theoretically illustrates the negative impacts of stop-and-go traffic on DRL training, and points to
22
the effectiveness of simplifying complex training environments, variance-reduction sampling
23
techniques, and regularization in ensuring training stability.
24
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces persistent
25
excitation and its correlation with stop-and-go traffic. Sections 3 and 4 theoretically illustrate the
26
impacts of stop-and-go traffic on BC-based and DRL-based LCCs, respectively. Section 5
27
validates the findings using numerical experiments. Section 6 provides concluding comments
28
and discusses future directions.

6 Concluding Comments
1
This study leverages the PE condition in system identification to theoretically analyze the
2
impacts of stop-and-go traffic on the generalizability of BC-based and DRL-based LCCs in
3
different traffic conditions. The theoretical analysis and numerical experiments indicate that: (i)
4
high-variance stop-and-go traffic can enhance the convergence of CF parameter estimation and
5
the generalizability of BC-based LCC in different traffic conditions; (ii) high-variance stop-and-
6
go traffic can induce large variance in DRL training, preventing DRL-based LCC from
7
converging to the optimal control policy and entailing additional laborious efforts to tune hyper-
8
parameters and shape the reward function. This also pinpoints the significance of variance-
9
reduction techniques such as importance sampling (Xie et al., 2019), dense DRL (Feng et al.,
10
2023), training regularization (Jin and Lavaei, 2020; Zhou et al., 2023a), and variance-reduction
11
baseline (Mao et al., 2018; Kumar et al., 2019); (iii) DRL-based LCC trained using low-variance
12
free-flow traffic covering all operating speeds can generalize to traffic congestion scenarios, as
13
the exploration mechanism in DRL enables the input signal used in DNNs to attain sufficient PE
14
order. This indicates that simplifying the training environment and reducing variance in samples
15
during DRL training can enhance convergence, while properly designing the reward function and
16
exploration mechanisms can improve optimality and generalizability.
17
The study insights illustrate that the generalizability of BC-based LCC entails the need for
18
comprehensive training datasets involving stop-and-go traffic with different characteristics,
19
inducing substantial computing and storage costs from CAV manufacturers. By contrast, DRL-
20
based LCC with proper exploration-exploitation and reward function can generalize to different
21
traffic conditions after training in simple free-flow traffic scenarios. Thereby, DRL is a more
22
cost-effective and promising LCC approach which can reduce the costs of CAV deployment and
23
operations, and enable CAVs to mitigate traffic congestion, enhance safety, and improve
24
efficiency in real-world traffic with different characteristics (e.g., in different cities and/or at
25
different time of a day). This can significantly aid the consideration and adoption of CAVs by
26
road users and foster rapid scale up in the future.
27
The insights from this study suggest the following future research directions: (i)
28
investigating the impacts of stop-and-go traffic on BC-based and DRL-based LCCs using other
29
DNN structures (e.g., convolutional NN, recurrent NN, transformer network, etc.); (ii) designing
30
variance-reduction sampling technique and adaptive exploration mechanism to balance
31
exploration-exploitation for optimality; (iii) analyzing the linkage between indirect adaptive
32
control and DRL, and further incorporating control-theoretic constraints to strictly guarantee
33
training stability and optimality; (iv) extending the analysis of generalizability to adversarial
34
training factoring sensing noise and control execution error; and (v) exploring online DRL to
35
enhance the adaptive performance in real-world traffic with rapidly evolving dynamics.
