As autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles will coexist for several dec-
ades, how to efﬁciently manage the mixed trafﬁc, while enhancing road
throughput, fuel consumption and trafﬁc stability becomes a challenge. This is
due to the randomness and heterogeneity of traditional vehicles interspersed
among autonomous vehicles. Moreover, communication delays arising from
the shared wireless communication network substantially degrade the per-
formance of platooning control for connected autonomous vehicles. To
address these challenging problems, this paper proposes a knowledge-guided
self-learning mixed platoon control strategy. Firstly, the proposed strategy
extracts key features of the continuous and aggregated behavior of traditional
vehicles, such as desired time-varying time gap and standstill spacing, by
integrating knowledge from the kinematic wave model and Newell’s car-
following model. This helps autonomous vehicles predict traditional vehicles’
trajectories. Secondly, to tackle delayed current state information, the study
incorporates previous control instructions into the state representation of the
soft actor-critic algorithm. Simulations show the proposed strategy outper-
forms existing methods in trafﬁc stability, passenger comfort, energy con-
sumption cost and trafﬁc oscillation dampening, with a zero collision rate in
vehicle merging and diverging scenarios. The framework provides a general-
izable and scalable solution for the development and adoption of connected
autonomous vehicle systems.
The rapid growth of autonomous vehicles is placing signiﬁcant strain
on global trafﬁc management systems, due to the complexities of
mixed-trafﬁc environments and the unpredictable nature of vehicle
behavior1,2. Research suggests that by 2050, fully autonomous vehi-
cles, classiﬁed as SAE (Society of Automotive Engineers) Level 5
Autonomous Driving, may account for between 10% and 71% of total
vehicle travel, and comprise 90% of vehicle sales3,4. This suggests that
autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles will coexist for several
decades5–7. The effectiveness of future trafﬁc and safety control
systems will hinge on their ability to manage this mixed trafﬁc efﬁ-
ciently, while also addressing trafﬁc congestion, improving safety
performance8,9, reducing greenhouse gas emissions and energy
consumption10,11. Unlike autonomous vehicles, which operate based on
predeﬁned algorithms and sensor inputs, traditional vehicles are
controlled by human drivers whose behaviors can be highly unpre-
dictable. Autonomous vehicles adhere to strict control protocols,
allowing for precise and consistent actions. In contrast, human-driven
vehicles introduce variability and complexity into trafﬁc systems due

to their tendency to deviate from optimal driving patterns12–15. This
difference in behavior is a signiﬁcant challenge for trafﬁc management
systems, as it affects the stability and efﬁciency of trafﬁc ﬂow. For
instance, as shown in Fig. 1b, sudden braking or acceleration by a
human-driven vehicle can cause trafﬁc oscillations, impacting the
performance of platoons and increasing the risk of collisions16–18.
To address the challenges of mixed trafﬁc, platooning control
techniques have emerged as a promising solution19. Platooning
involves the coordination of multiple vehicles to travel closely
together at a consistent speed, reducing the impact of individual
vehicle actions on trafﬁc ﬂow. The primary objective of platooning
control is to ensure that all vehicles maintain the same velocity while
keeping a safe inter-vehicular distance. However, implementing
effective platooning control in mixed trafﬁc environments is extre-
mely complex. The integration of autonomous vehicles and tradi-
tional vehicles requires sophisticated control algorithms that can
accommodate the unpredictable behavior of human-driven vehicles
and the communication delays inherent in the shared wireless
communication system.
A signiﬁcant challenge in designing platooning controllers is
accounting for the unpredictable behavior of individual traditional
vehicles interspersed among connected autonomous vehicles. Some
related studies estimate the behavior of traditional vehicles using
statistical methods20,21, but these methods are limited to trafﬁc mon-
itoring purposes because of the lack of an explicit model. The study
referenced in22 analyzes the interactions between classical vehicles and
autonomous vehicles with the car-following experiment data, and
highlights the importance of randomness and heterogeneity of
human-driving behaviors when designing mixed-trafﬁc platooning
algorithms, but it does not incorporate controller design. In the fol-
lowing, we limit ourselves to the literature on platooning control
strategies for vehicle platoons only, which are most relevant to the
current work.
Based on methodological differences, state-of-the-art algorithms
for mixed vehicle platooning control can be divided into those based
on linear and nonlinear control23–31, optimization control15,16,32–36, and
deep reinforcement learning (DRL) based control37–40.
Researchers have designed the platooning controller based on the
linear and nonlinear control theory, such as the H∞control and sliding
mode control23–31. Based on the differences in control objectives, the
above controllers can be divided into predictive control-based
controller23–28 and robust controller29–31. Predictive control optimizes
control inputs by forecasting the future states of the system to achieve
speciﬁc performance metrics, such as minimizing prediction errors.
This approach prioritizes achieving high system performance through
prediction and optimization. In contrast, robust control emphasizes
maintaining system stability and reliability under uncertainties. For
instance, we designed some kinds of robust controllers to guarantee
the stability of a vehicle platoon in the presence of time delays and
external disturbances in our paper29–31. While effective in simple sce-
narios, their model-dependent nature can lead to signiﬁcant perfor-
mance issues in real and complex scenarios. Thus, effectively
managing mixed trafﬁc with high variability remains a challenging task.
Optimization control methods, such as Model Predictive Control
(MPC), have been used to compute real-time vehicle control
commands15,16,32–36. However, MPC usually requires convex problems,
and its complex solving formulas result in a large computational load
and poor real-time performance. To address this issue, references34–36
propose optimal longitudinal control algorithms for connected
autonomous vehicles in mixed platoons. These algorithms aim to
minimize the deviations of vehicle headway and velocity in the pre-
sence of wireless communication delays, but they are model-
dependent and require the drivers’ behavior to be homogeneous.
DRL offers signiﬁcant advantages, such as simplifying the need for
complex model construction and enabling real-time operation. While
prior research has extensively applied DRL to enhance the safety,
energy efﬁciency, and driving comfort of vehicle platoons41–46, these
methods, primarily designed for platoons consisting solely of con-
nected autonomous vehicles, are not optimized for the current
mixed trafﬁc where autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles
share the road. In other words, the exploration of DRL in mixed pla-
toons is still in ﬁrst stages. Utilizing typical DRL algorithms like Prox-
imal Policy Optimization (PPO)37, and Deep Deterministic Policy
Gradient (DDPG)38, researchers have made strides in optimizing the

To address the issue of communication delay, an intelligent control

multiple traditional vehicles. The primary distinctions between40 and
our work are two-fold: ﬁrst, the assumption that the desired inter-

are available has been relied upon in ref. 40. This assumption under-
lying the algorithm in ref. 40 limits its application in practice, since
accurately measuring these information of human-driven vehicles is
challenging due to their limited communication capability. Second,
whereas the communication delay considered in ref. 40 is assumed to
be constant, while the proposed algorithm in our study is designed to
handle varying delays, making it more adaptable to real-world condi-
tions. Therefore, developing a controller capable of managing mixed
trafﬁc conditions, where both autonomous vehicles and traditional
vehicles share the road, while simultaneously considering the random
actions of human-driven vehicles and communication delays, remains
an open problem.
This paper aims to ﬁll this gap by presenting a knowledge-guided
self-learning framework. This framework not only accounts for the
unpredictable behavior of traditional vehicles but also adapts to
varying trafﬁc conditions and random communication delays, ensur-
ing robust performance across different scenarios. The proposed fra-
mework is built on the vehicle-road-cloud system, as presented in
Fig. 2, where the cloud platform and connected autonomous vehicles
exchange real-time data through the vehicle-to-everything (V2X)
communication network, facilitated by the roadside unites, and the
cloud controller generates the control command for connected
autonomous vehicles47. The details of this framework will be elabo-
rated in the following section.
Compared with the results in the published literature, the prin-
cipal contributions of this research are outlined as follows:
To address the limitation of current work that does not incorpo-
rate controller design into the analysis of interactions between classi-
cal vehicles and autonomous vehicles, we propose a generic
knowledge-guided self-learning platooning control framework. This
framework is designed to be robust regardless of the number of
human-driven vehicles and random communication delays, while also
meeting the real-time requirements of future transportation systems.
Aiming at the problem that current research relies on the known
behavior of each human-driven vehicle, which are challenging to pre-
dict due to the randomness inherent in traditional vehicles, this paper
ﬁrst characterizes the continuous and aggregated classical vehicles
insert among connected autonomous vehicles as a single entity. This
characterization helps in predicting the aggregated behavior of tradi-
tional vehicles and their impact on the overall trafﬁc dynamics. Then
estimates the aggregated behavior of classical vehicles, such as desired
time-varying time gaps and desired standstill spacing information, by
incorporating the kinematic wave model and the Newell’s car-
following model, which presents the linear relationship between
standstill spacing and the kinematic wave velocity-with the prior trafﬁc
information. Moreover, by integrating prior knowledge such as the
kinematic wave model and Newell’s car-following model into the
learning process, we can guide the learning more effectively and
improve efﬁciency.
Considering the causality of the system–where the current state is
inﬂuenced by its previous states and previous inputs–we incorporate
the previous control input into the state representation of the SAC
method. This design guides the training process and leverages the high
computational performance and storage capacity of the cloud server.

Results in Results section demonstrate that the proposed

Knowledge-guided self-learning control framework
The knowledge-guided self-learning mixed platoon control frame-
work, as depicted in Fig. 2, ﬁrstly decomposes the mixed platoon into
distinct sub-platoons in the “CV-HVs-CV" (i.e., leading connected
autonomous vehicles followed by traditional vehicles and then con-
nected autonomous vehicles) formation (shown in Fig. 1c). Secondly,
incorporates the aggregated model of classical vehicles as a
knowledge-guided module, which is designed to predict the desired
time-varying time gaps and desired standstill space of aggregated
human-driven
vehicles.
Thirdly,
consider
the
previous
control
instructions in the state representation of the SAC, and design a model
switching mechanism to compensate for the performance degradation
caused by random communication delays. Finally, the intelligent soft
actor-critic (SAC) algorithm is used as the data-driven module of the
system to maximize the car-following and comfort rewards for the
connected autonomous vehicles and verify the effectiveness of the
control framework in various scenarios.
The advantages of using cloud computing are twofold: it provides
high-performance computing resources that support real-time deci-
sion-making for complex control algorithms, and it offers elastic sto-
rage space capable of storing multiple neural network models,
enabling rapid loading and access.
Our mixed platoon control framework implements an end-to-end
control strategy, as it maps the information gathered from roadside
units and the V2X wireless communication network directly to control
commands, as detailed in Fig. 2. Speciﬁcally, the cloud serves as the
central processing unit, handling computational tasks such as data
processing and control command generation. This high-performance
capability ensures that complex control algorithms can be executed in
real-time, providing dynamic and responsive control strategies. In
parallel, roadside units are responsible for collecting macroscopic
trafﬁc information (such as trafﬁc wave speed and jam density) from
the current road segment47. Through V2X communication, as well as
vehicle-mounted radar sensors, connected autonomous vehicles
interact both with each other, the cloud and roadside units, facilitating
real-time data exchange. Upon gathering vehicle and trafﬁc data,
roadside units forward this information to the cloud’s self-learning
controller. The controller then computes the appropriate longitudinal
control commands for each autonomous vehicle, tailoring them to the
speciﬁc conditions of the road and trafﬁc. These commands are
relayed back to the respective autonomous vehicles via the V2X net-
work. The elastic storage space provided by the cloud allows for the
storage of multiple neural network models, enabling the controller to
switch between different models based on real-time conditions,
thereby optimizing performance.
Results in Fig. 3 show that, under the guidance of the cloud con-
troller, the connected vehicle driving strategy integrated with the
intelligent driver model (CVDS-IDM) strategies in ref. 48, DDPG control
in ref. 40, and the proposed strategy enables platoons consisting of
connected autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles to closely
track an aggressive leading vehicle with minimal trafﬁc oscillation and
a comfortable driving experience. Moreover, the proposed strategy
outperforms PPO control in ref. 37, DDPG control in ref. 40, actor-critic
(AC) control in ref. 44, and the CVDS-IDM strategies in ref. 48 in terms
of trafﬁc stability, passenger comfort, energy consumption cost, and
trafﬁc oscillation dampening performance. These results highlight the
feasibility of our self-learning method, demonstrating its applicability
across various trafﬁc system conditions.
Comparison results between different control algorithms
To demonstrate the practicability and effectiveness of the proposed
control framework, this section presents numerical simulation exam-
ples. These examples are designed to simulate the dynamic behaviorof
the mixed platoon comprising seven vehicles, as depicted in Fig. 3a,
where the leading vehicle, as well as the 2-nd and 6-th following vehi-
cles, are designated as connected autonomous vehicles, while the
remaining vehicles are classiﬁed as traditional vehicles. The leading
vehicle serves as the tracking target. Guided by the NGSIM data49, it
follows a low-velocity aggressive driving style characterized by low
velocity and signiﬁcant volatility in acceleration and deceleration. This
driving style is representative of the behavior exhibited by most
human drivers in real-world scenarios22,43,50. Besides the leading vehi-
cle, all autonomous vehicles are controlled by the proposed strategy.
And traditional vehicles are governed by the optimal velocity model
used in refs. 36,44, which is one of the most popular models to capture
the car-following behaviors of classical vehicles. For the sake of con-
sistency and reproducibility, the default experimental settings for this
study are outlined in Supplementary Table 2, offering a foundation for
the simulation parameters used.
The initial velocity of all vehicles is set to 11m/s, and each vehicle
maintains a speciﬁed distance from the preceding vehicle. The com-
munication delay is set to a constant value of 20 ms by default. Due to
the limited availability of related research, we compare the proposed
knowledge-guided self-learning SAC strategy with several existing
methods in the mixed platoon context, including PPO37, DDPG40, AC44,
and CVDS-IDM strategies48. This comparison aims to further demon-
strate the superiority of the proposed strategy. The signal phases and
timing of the intersection are considered in ref. 37, and the impacts of
trafﬁc signals are incorporated into the reward function design to
optimize the trajectories of connected autonomous vehicles. It
requires exact desired inter-vehicular distance and velocity informa-
tion for human-driven vehicles in ref. 40. A cooperative multi-agent
actor-critic scheme is developed to optimize connected autonomous
vehicles platoon management policies via the AC architecture in
ref. 44. The IDM with estimation errors is utilized to model traditional
vehicles and the CVDS-IDM is develpoed to model connected auto-
momous vehicles48. It incorporates human factors such as estimation
errors and driver compliance, which are critical for the success of
connected autonomous vehicles. The assumptions underlying these
methods differ from those of this paper. To address the problem
considered in this paper, we have extended PPO37, DDPG40, AC44, and
CVDS-IDM strategies48 to ﬁt the proposed framework and have made
appropriate modiﬁcations to them. And the default experimental set-
tings are the same as those of the proposed strategy.

The comparative analysis of various algorithms given in Fig. 3
reveals signiﬁcant differences in their performance in terms of platoon
tracking stability and control effectiveness. Traditional DRL algo-
rithms, such as PPO and AC, exhibit considerable instability in platoon
tracking, with abrupt changes in acceleration and inconsistent speed
control. As shown by the position curves of Fig. 3c and d, collisions
even occur during operation, highlighting the limitations of these
algorithms in providing stable and effective control. In contrast, the
proposed algorithm demonstrates exceptional performance (Fig. 3e),
with stable platoon progression and smooth acceleration changes,
thereby achieving superior control stability and overall performance.
We also use Fig. 4 to illustrate the performance differences among
various algorithms across four key metrics. Figure 4 presents an ana-
lysis of vehicle performance based on the average value of each time
step across various indicators. In Fig. 4a, the cumulative damping ratio
of the knowledge-guided self-learning SAC strategy exhibits a
decreasing trend, dropping from 0.7 to 0.54. This trend indicates that
the controller is effective at suppressing trafﬁc oscillations under
varying conditions and outperforms the other strategies. Fig. 4b and c
demonstrate that the knowledge-guided self-learning SAC strategy
signiﬁcantly outperforms the other four strategies in terms of driving
comfort and energy consumption, respectively. Furthermore, the
analysis reveals that traditional vehicles (i.e., vehicles with numbers 3
to 5) tend to exacerbate the propagation of interference within the
trafﬁc ﬂow, while connected autonomous vehicles (i.e., the 2-nd and
6-th vehicles) are capable of mitigating such interference. Lastly,
Fig. 4d illustrates that the trafﬁc ﬂow stability achieved by the pro-
posed strategy is superior.

Robustness validation
Robustness against the variations of communication delay. Figure 6
shows the speciﬁc driving curve of connected autonomous vehicles
with indexes 2 and 6 under different communication delays, with
values in the set {0.2ΔT, 0.5ΔT, 1.0ΔT, 2.0ΔT} where ΔT = 0.1s. Figure 6
demonstrates that the proposed strategy enables connected autono-
mous vehicles to maintain robustness against changes in commu-
nication delay. For instance, at approximately 12 s, the acceleration
amplitude of the second vehicle reaches nearly 1.5, which is the max-

Discussion
The key challenge in the development of the platooning controller
for mixed trafﬁc stems from the challenge of considering the ran-
domness of human driving behaviors and the random time delay in
the actual communication environment. We address these chal-
lenges with three aspects of our knowledge-guided self-learning
SAC framework. First, this study introduces an approach by inte-
grating the cloud to be the controller, thereby maximizing the uti-
lization of trafﬁc information, which holds signiﬁcant practical
application value. This is different from previous studies on mixed
platoons, which have often overlooked the potential of cloud
computing and roadside units, leading to sub-optimal use of com-
munication resources15,16,36. Secondly, we embed the kinematic wave
model and Newell’s car-following model as a knowledge-guided
module into the SAC algorithm to estimate the overall behavior of
traditional vehicles. This integration aims to mitigate the inter-
ference caused by the randomness inherent in each human-driven
vehicle and enhance the overall stability of the platoon. Third, to
address the inherent random delays in wireless networks, we inte-
grated previous control instructions, which are induced by the
delay, into the state representation and design a model switching
mechanism. Lastly, the number of classical vehicles in training is
variable, and the extended scenarios have been studied, enriching
the
empirical
data
and
broadening
the
model’s
application
scenarios.
The performance of the proposed control strategy is assessed by
benchmarking it against other DRL-based control37,40,44 and CVDS-IDM
control48 methods. The evaluation criteria include trafﬁc stability,
driving comfort, energy consumption cost, trafﬁc oscillation dam-
pening performance, and robustness against disturbances caused by
the lane-changing behavior of a human-driven vehicle. The results
suggest that the proposed control strategy signiﬁcantly outperforms
other strategies in every aspect and can greatly suppress the trafﬁc
oscillations, demonstrating its robustness and resilience against
disturbances.
This research demonstrates the potential of employing the pro-
posed data-driven strategy, in conjunction with a switching model
approach, to manage random communication delays effectively. In
addition, the generalizability of the proposed strategy has been con-
ﬁrmed through extensive testing with the NGSIM datasets and a variety
of customized trafﬁc scenarios, further validating its practical applic-
ability in real-world conditions.
Future research could focus on applying the proposed framework
to real-world vehicle platoons, further enhancing the robustness and
adaptability of the control strategy.
Methods
Assumptions
In this manuscript, an inﬁnite single straight highway is considered,
and the lateral motion of the vehicle is ignored. A connected autono-
mous vehicle is equipped with V2X communication technology to
communicate with other autonomous vehicles and roadside units. The
basic assumptions for the concerned mixed vehicle platoon are given
as follows:
• Each connected autonomous vehicle is equipped with an onboard
radar sensor to measure the real-time inter-vehicular spacing (i.e.,
the distance between front bumpers of the connected autono-
mous vehicle and its predecessor), and to measure the velocity
information of its predecessor, when its predecessor is a
traditional vehicle.
• The traditional vehicle’s real-time state information including
position, velocity, acceleration or desired headway to its preced-
ing vehicle (i.e., the desired time interval between the moment of
the front bumper of the preceding vehicle passes a given point on
the road and the moment the front bumper of the following
traditional vehicle reaches the same location) cannot be broad-
cast to other connected autonomous vehicles or human-driven
vehicles, due to their limited communication capability.
• Each connected autonomous vehicle’s real-time state information
(including position, speed, and acceleration), and measurement
information can be broadcast to the cloud platform through V2X
communication and roadside units.
• There exists a communication delay when the control command
information is transmitted from the cloud platform to the actua-
tor of the connected autonomous vehicle.
• Roadside units collect the real-time trafﬁc ﬂow information
including the trafﬁc ﬂow wave speed and jam density, and
forward them to the cloud.
Mixed platoon scenario
Regulating the longitudinal behavior of connected autonomous vehi-
cles in a mixed platoon is essential to dampen trafﬁc oscillations
caused by the randomness and heterogeneity of traditional vehicles’
behavior. To improve ride comfort and mitigate trafﬁc oscillations, the
ﬁrst step in our study is to decompose the mixed platoon into sub-
platoons with the car-following structure of “CV-HVs-CV". This struc-
ture is practical for real-world scenarios where connected autonomous
vehicles and traditional vehicles coexist, and serves as a versatile unit
for mixed platoons with various vehicle combinations, as depicted in
Fig. 11. In this structure, the leading autonomous vehicle (indexed by
i −n) is guided by the real-world data, while the following connected
autonomous vehicle (indexed by i) is the vehicle that needs to be
controlled. In the following subsection, we present the traditional
approaches to modeling the car-following behavior of connected
autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles. This presentation aims
to highlight the necessity of developing data-driven dynamics models
for mixed platoons.

ensure the optimal performance of the connected autonomous

Data preparation. In this study, we utilize the Next Generation
Simulation (NGSIM) data from the California I-80 project for our
tests49. This dataset is chosen due to the frequent trafﬁc con-
gestion and oscillation observed during peak hours, which are
critical for our analysis. Speciﬁcally, we focus on the vehicle
trajectory data captured between 4: 00 pm and 4: 15 pm, a
period characterized by relatively fewer lane change maneuvers,
thus providing a stable dataset for our experiments. To enhance
the quality of the data for analysis, the trajectories are recon-
structed using a low-pass ﬁlter. This ﬁltering process is essential
for smoothing out the noise and interference present in the
original data. Supplementary Fig. 2 demonstrates the effective-
ness of the low-pass ﬁlter in reﬁning the data, ensuring that it is
suitable for our research purposes.
Training procedure. Figure 11 illustrates the knowledge-guided self-
learning mixed platoon control framework based on the SAC algo-
rithm. In the left side, the framework incorporates a prior trafﬁc
information as a knowledge-guided module, which is designed to
predict the overall behavior of aggregated traditional vehicles. The
SAC algorithm is deployed as the data-driven module, which con-
tinuously interacts with the environment. The number of classical
vehicles in training is variable, enriching the empirical data and
broadening the model’s application scenarios. Instead of a ﬁxed
deterministic leader, NGSIM data is utilized to represent the leader’s
behavior, the rationality of which has been validated in previous
studies43,50.

The middle of Fig. 11 depicts the structure of the neural network.
During training, the SAC algorithm continuously interacts with the
trafﬁc environment, generating actions to apply to the system model.
The state of the system is inﬂuenced by the current vehicle’s error state
